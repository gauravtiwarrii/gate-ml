GATE — Common Section

General Aptitude (15 Marks)

Comprehensive preparation guide covering English grammar, verbal deduction, numerical computation, and data interpretation for the GATE examination.

Official GATE General Aptitude Pattern: 10 questions carrying 15 total marks (5 questions × 1 mark + 5 questions × 2 marks). Common across all GATE question papers.

1. The Strategic Importance of General Aptitude

General Aptitude (GA) constitutes 15% of your total GATE score. Because technical engineering mathematics and core computer science subjects carry high difficulty, General Aptitude is widely recognized as the section offering the highest return on study time. Top rankers consistently score 12 to 14+ marks out of 15 in this section.

3. Verbal Aptitude: High-Frequency Grammar & Logic

  • Subject-Verb Agreement: Singular subjects take singular verbs; plural subjects take plural verbs. Pay close attention to collective nouns, phrases connected by neither/nor (the verb agrees with the closer subject), and intervening prepositional phrases (e.g., "The quality of these products is high", not are).
  • Tense Consistency & Conditionals:
    • Zero Conditional (general truths): If + present, present (e.g., If water reaches 100°C, it boils).
    • First Conditional (real possibilities): If + present, will + base verb.
    • Second Conditional (hypothetical): If + past simple, would + base verb (e.g., If I were you, I would study).
    • Third Conditional (past counterfactual): If + past perfect, would have + past participle.
  • Critical Reasoning & Syllogisms: Identify the premise, the stated evidence, and the conclusion. Distinguish between an assumption (an unstated prerequisite) and an inference (a logically unavoidable deduction).

4. Quantitative Aptitude: Essential Arithmetic & Algebraic Rules

  • Percentages & Successive Changes: Successive percentage changes of +a\% and +b\% yield a net change of (a + b + \frac{ab}{100})\%.
  • Ratios & Proportions: If A:B = 2:3 and B:C = 4:5, combine via common B: A:B:C = (2 \times 4) : (3 \times 4) : (3 \times 5) = 8 : 12 : 15.
  • Work, Rate & Time: If worker A completes a job in d_A days and B in d_B days, their combined rate is \frac{1}{d_A} + \frac{1}{d_B}, completing the job together in \frac{d_A d_B}{d_A + d_B} days.
  • Speed, Distance & Relative Velocity:
    • Average speed for two equal distances covered at speeds v_1 and v_2 is the Harmonic Mean: v_{avg} = \frac{2 v_1 v_2}{v_1 + v_2} (not the arithmetic mean!).
    • Two objects moving in opposite directions have relative speed v_1 + v_2; in the same direction, relative speed is |v_1 - v_2|.

5. Data Interpretation & Spatial Reasoning

  • Percentage Increase vs. Percentage Points: Moving from 20% to 25% is an increase of 5 percentage points, but represents a \frac{25 - 20}{20} \times 100\% = 25\% percentage increase.
  • Spatial Visualization: Mental folding of paper patterns (cubes from nets), mirror reflections across vertical/horizontal axes, and rotational symmetry (90° and 180° transforms).

6. Worked Step-by-Step Examples

Worked Example 1: Quantitative Problem (Harmonic Speed Trap)

Problem: A student travels from home to campus at a speed of 30 km/h and returns along the exact same route at 60 km/h. What is the average speed for the entire round trip?

Solution:

Let the one-way distance be d km.

Time for outward trip: t_1 = d / 30 hours.
Time for return trip: t_2 = d / 60 hours.
Total distance: 2d km.
Total time: t_1 + t_2 = \frac{d}{30} + \frac{d}{60} = \frac{3d}{60} = \frac{d}{20} hours.

Average speed: v_{avg} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} = \frac{2d}{d / 20} = 40\text{ km/h}.

(Notice: The naive arithmetic average (30 + 60)/2 = 45 km/h is a classic GATE distractor trap!).

Worked Example 2: Verbal Syllogism & Deduction

Problem: Given statements: (1) All algorithms are procedures. (2) Some procedures are heuristics. Can we conclude that "Some algorithms are heuristics"?

Solution:

Let A = Algorithms, P = Procedures, H = Heuristics.

Statement 1 implies A \subseteq P. Statement 2 implies P \cap H \ne \emptyset.

However, the intersection between P and H can lie entirely outside the subset A. Therefore, the conclusion does not necessarily follow from the premises.

7. GATE Exam Traps & Time Management Rules

  • Pacing Rule: Allocate exactly 20 to 25 minutes for the 10 General Aptitude questions at the beginning of your test session. Getting these marks secured early builds momentum for the technical section.
  • Negative Marking Discipline: Do not guess on 1-mark (⅓ penalty) or 2-mark (⅔ penalty) MCQs. On NAT questions, there is zero penalty, so always provide an informed calculation.
